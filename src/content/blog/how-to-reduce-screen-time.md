## Is phone addiction real?

"Phone addiction" isn't a formal medical diagnosis, and most people who say they're addicted to their phone don't mean it clinically. They mean something simpler: they pick up their phone without deciding to, they stay longer than they meant to, and they feel worse afterward.

That pattern is real, and it's not a willpower failure. Feeds, autoplay, infinite scroll and short-form video are designed so there's never a natural place to stop. If stopping is always *your* job, the app usually wins.

If your phone use is seriously affecting your sleep, work, relationships or mood, it's worth talking to a doctor or therapist as well. The tools below help with habits. They're not a replacement for real support.

## Why "just use your phone less" doesn't work

Most attempts to cut screen time fail in one of three ways:

1. **The goal is too vague.** "Less phone" gives you nothing to act on at 11pm when you're already scrolling.
2. **The limit is too strict.** Going from four hours a day to thirty minutes feels great on Monday. By Thursday you've switched it off.
3. **The bypass is too easy.** If getting past a limit is one tap, the tap becomes a reflex. After a week you don't even see the warning any more.

The fix for each one is the same idea: make the habit **visible**, make the change **small**, and make the bypass a **real decision**. We go deeper on the third point in [why most app blockers stop working after a week](../why-app-blockers-stop-working/).

## A 7-step plan to reduce screen time

### 1. Measure for a few days before changing anything

Don't set any limits yet. Just watch where your time goes for two or three days.

On Android you can see this in **Settings → Digital Wellbeing**. In Curbox, the **Usage** tab shows your total screen time today, a weekly chart, a per-app list, and which websites you spent time on in supported browsers. You can also export it all as a CSV.

Most people are surprised. The app that takes the most time is often not the one they would have guessed.

### 2. Put your screen time where you'll see it

Awareness on its own changes behavior more than you'd expect. Put a screen time widget on your home screen so the number is the first thing you see when you unlock your phone. Curbox has a **Screentime Widget** for total time today and a **Reels Widget** for short videos watched today.

For some people, this step alone is enough.

### 3. Limit one app, not your whole phone

Pick the single app that surprised you most and give it one limit. Set it close to what you use now, not what you wish you used. If you spend 90 minutes a day on Instagram, start at 75, not 15.

In Curbox this is **App Pause**, which can limit an app in three ways:

- **Usage based:** a daily cap, like 45 minutes. Resets at midnight.
- **Time based:** the app only works in a window you pick, like after 6pm.
- **On each open:** a pause screen every single time you open it.

Next week, lower the limit a little. Small changes you keep beat big changes you abandon.

### 4. Make the bypass a real decision

This is the step that decides whether any of this lasts. When you hit a limit, you need *some* way past it (life happens), but it should take enough effort that you notice you're choosing.

Curbox's **Unlock Challenges** control what it takes to continue past a limit:

| Challenge | What it does | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wait to unlock** | A countdown before you can continue. Fixed, or random so you can't predict it. | Breaking autopilot gently. |
| **Type a sentence** | You have to type a specific sentence exactly. | Apps you sometimes need. |
| **State your intent** | You type why you're opening the app. Your reasons are saved for later. | Seeing your own patterns. |
| **QR or barcode scan** | You scan a code you set up in advance. | Making it physically inconvenient. |
| **Never unlock** | No way past until the limit resets. | The one app you can't moderate. |

A trick that works well: register the barcode on a cereal box or a book in another room as your unlock code. By the time you've walked over there, you often don't want the app any more.

You can also add a **Proceed Limit**, which caps how many times a day you can get past a block at all.

### 5. Target the addictive part, not the whole app

Deleting Instagram works until someone messages you on Instagram. Deleting YouTube works until you need a tutorial. The part that eats your evening is usually one feature, most often short-form video.

So limit that feature directly:

- **Short-Form Video Content** in Curbox limits Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and Facebook Reels by daily time or by number of videos. The count is **combined across apps**, so you can't just switch from Reels to Shorts.
- **Short-Form Video Counter** shows a live count on screen while you scroll. Seeing "47" is surprisingly effective.
- **UI Hider** (in beta) hides distracting parts of apps while leaving the rest usable: the Instagram home feed except Following, the YouTube recommendations feed, or the "For You" tab on X.

### 6. Close the loopholes

Habits move. Limit the Instagram app and the website is still in your browser. Limit your phone during the day and it moves to bed.

- **Browsers:** Curbox's **Keywords & Websites** blocker watches the address bar in Chrome, Brave, Firefox, Fennec, Opera, Cromite and Vanadium, so the website version of a limited app isn't an easy way round. On a computer, there's a Curbox [browser extension](https://curbox.app/download.html) for Chromium browsers and Firefox.
- **Night time:** use a time-based App Pause rule so distracting apps stop working at bedtime, and **Auto DND** to turn on Do Not Disturb on a schedule.
- **Color:** the **Grayscale Filter** turns chosen apps black and white. Without bright colors, feeds are a lot easier to put down. (This one needs the free helper app Shizuku.)

### 7. Use nudges, not just walls

Not every app needs to be blocked. For apps you use a bit too much, a gentle prompt is often enough.

Curbox's **Mindful Messages** shows a note you wrote to yourself on top of an app while you use it. It can include live numbers:

```text
You've been here {live_session_duration}. Still worth it?
```

Other placeholders include `{app_usage_today}`, `{screentime_today}` and `{reel_count}`. It doesn't stop you. It just makes you look at what you're doing.

## For work and study: use focus sessions

Daily limits are for habits. When you just need the next hour to go well, use a timed focus session instead.

Curbox's **Focus Mode** lets you pick a length and a group of apps and websites to block, then start. You can also flip it around with **Block All Except Selected**, so only the apps you pick (maps, music, notes) work. Twenty-five minutes is a good first session. Focus Stats tracks your sessions, completion rate and streak.

## FAQ

### How can I reduce my screen time quickly?

Find your single most-used app and add a limit with an unlock challenge, like a 10-second wait or typing a sentence. Put a screen time widget on your home screen. Those two changes take five minutes and make a noticeable difference in the first week.

### What is the best app to reduce screen time on Android?

For a free option, Curbox is a strong choice: it's open source, has no ads or subscriptions, and combines app limits, Reels and Shorts blocking, website blocking, focus sessions and usage stats. Android's built-in Digital Wellbeing is a good starting point if you only need simple app timers.

### How do I stop being addicted to my phone?

Treat it as a habit, not a character flaw. Measure first, change one thing at a time, add friction right before the habit (not after), and target the specific feature that hooks you, like short-form video. If it's seriously affecting your life, talk to a professional too.

### How do I stop scrolling Reels and Shorts?

Limit short-form video specifically instead of deleting the apps. Curbox can block Reels and Shorts after a set time or number of videos, combined across Instagram, YouTube and Facebook, while keeping messages and regular videos working.

### How much screen time is too much?

There's no single number that's right for everyone, and time spent on maps, messages or work isn't the same as time spent on an endless feed. A more useful question is whether your phone use matches what you'd choose if you thought about it. If it doesn't, that's worth changing.

### Can I reduce screen time without deleting apps?

Yes. Limits, time windows, unlock challenges and hiding parts of apps (like feeds) let you keep the useful parts of an app while cutting the parts that take your time.
